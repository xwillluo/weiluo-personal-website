I am Wei Luo, a sociologist and Assistant Professor at the School of Government, Peking University. Working across contemporary and historical China, I bring organization theory into conversation with public administration, economic sociology, and comparative historical sociology.

How organizations are built, and how they reach beyond themselves

Organizations depend on more than formal rules, structures, and authority. I study how they draw on social relationships to become capable of acting.

I first pursued this question historically. My book project traces the parallel development of family and banking practices in inland China from the late nineteenth to the mid twentieth century. It examines how modern financial organizations were assembled through existing social relations, rather than emerging only by replacing them.

My later research moved from how organizations emerge to how they operate within complex environments. Studying civic organizations in contemporary urban China led me to examine how organizations navigate relationships among the state, market, and communities. My current work goes further inside these processes, asking how public agencies and civic organizations mobilize people, relationships, and technologies beyond their formal boundaries, and how doing so redistributes labor, authority, risk, and responsibility.

Across historical and contemporary settings, my research examines how organizations acquire capacities that their formal structures alone cannot provide.

Research Fields

My research follows organizations across historical periods, urban settings, and institutional boundaries. Across these settings, I examine how social relationships become sources of organizational capacity and how this process reshapes authority, labor, risk, and responsibility.

  • Civic and Nonprofit Organizations: I study how civic and nonprofit organizations navigate relationships with governments, markets, members, and communities. My work examines how they secure resources, respond to regulation, represent diverse constituencies, and turn civic participation into organizational capacity.
  • Global Cities and Comparative Governance: I examine how global competition, political institutions, technology, and local social structures shape urban governance. Through comparisons across cities and neighborhoods, I study how civic organizations and frontline actors translate policy ambitions into everyday practice.
  • Administrative Thought: This theme connects my historical interest in how organizations emerge with my contemporary research on governance. I examine how ideas about authority, coordination, responsibility, and public purpose arise from practical problems of organizing and shape how those problems are addressed.

Bridging Divides or Widening Gaps? Nonprofit Organizations’ Efforts for Migrant Inclusion in Two Global Cities

Yan Long, Wei Luo, Berta Terzieva
Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly, 55(4), 1049–1074, 2026 (first published online 2025).
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
Using organizational survey data from Vienna and Shenzhen, this study examines how nonprofit governance, staff composition, and interpersonal contact shape whether organizations include and serve migrants in proportion to their presence in local communities.
Journal 2026

Organizing for Crisis: The Shanghai Civic Association and the Formalization of Elite Voluntary Organizations, 1932–1937

Wei Luo
Twentieth-Century China, 51(2), 191–214, 2026.
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
Drawing on archival research, this article traces how the Shanghai Civic Association developed from an informal elite network responding to the 1932 Shanghai War into a formalized civic bureaucracy. Structured procedures, routinized practices, and stable decision-making enhanced its capacity to coordinate relief, mobilize resources, and supplement municipal governance.
Journal 2025

Affective Lockdown: Administrative Chaos and Informal Repairing in Urban China during COVID-19

Yan Long, Wei Luo
The China Journal, 93, 51–75, 2025.
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
Drawing on 50 interviews with frontline workers, this article shows how administrative breakdowns complicated targeted lockdowns and how workers used informal affective labor to secure residents’ cooperation and keep neighborhood governance functioning.
Journal 2024

Neighborhood Effects on Integrative Organizational Practices in Five Global Cities

Christof Brandtner, Krystal Laryea, Gowun Park, Wei Luo, Michael Meyer, David Suárez, Hokyu Hwang, Walter W. Powell
Nature Cities, 1(12), 853–860, 2024.
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
Combining organizational and neighborhood data from five global cities, this study shows that civic organizations’ integrative practices vary with neighborhood income and migrant composition. Social integration is more common in poorer neighborhoods, while systemic integration is strongest in affluent, heterogeneous neighborhoods.
Journal 2023

Taming Violence: The Shanghai Green Gang and its Self-Legitimation Claims in the Early Twentieth Century

Wei Luo
Social Science History, 47(4), 585–608, 2023.
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
This article examines how the Shanghai Green Gang responded to rapid social change by reframing its violence as disciplinary, revolutionary, and nationalistic. Its cultural work, alongside its roles in state building and resource extraction, helped legitimate the organization and expand its political influence.
Journal 2023

Relational Work and its Pitfalls: Nonprofits’ Participation in Government-Sponsored Voluntary Accreditation

Wei Luo, Wenjuan Zheng, Yan Long
Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 33(1), 63–79, 2023.
Dedicated Page & Summary DOI Link
Drawing on interviews and survey data from 203 nonprofits in Shenzhen, this study shows that government agencies use existing regulatory relationships to recruit organizations into voluntary accreditation. Targeting politically embedded nonprofits can encourage participation, but it can also deter it.
